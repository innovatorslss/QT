PATTERNS IN CRACKING

Investigation into how drying, thickness, temperature and material properties influence the formation and geometry of cracks.

1. KEY WORDS AND DEFINITIONS

Drying: Removal of water or another liquid from a material.
Shrinkage: Reduction in volume or dimensions as a material loses liquid.
Stress: Internal force acting over a given area of a material.
Strain: Deformation produced relative to the original size.
Tensile stress: Stress that tends to pull a material apart.
Fracture: Breaking of a material when applied or internal stress exceeds its strength.
Crack density: Number of cracks occurring within a given area.
Crack spacing: Distance between neighbouring cracks.

2. AIM

To investigate how drying conditions and the thickness of a drying material affect the formation, number, width and spacing of cracks.

Explanation:
The investigation was designed to determine why materials such as mud, clay and paint develop visible crack patterns during drying. When water leaves the material, the particles move closer together and the material attempts to shrink. If the material is attached to a surface that restricts this movement, internal stress develops. When the stress becomes greater than the strength of the material, cracks form.

3. HYPOTHESIS

If the drying rate and thickness of the material are increased, then the number and size of cracks will increase because faster moisture loss and greater shrinkage will produce greater internal tensile stress.

Reason for the hypothesis:
A thicker layer contains more material that must undergo shrinkage. Rapid drying can also create differences in moisture between the surface and deeper regions. These effects can increase stress and encourage fracture.

4. VARIABLES

Independent variables:
• Thickness of material
• Drying temperature
• Drying rate

Dependent variables:
• Number of cracks
• Crack width
• Crack spacing
• Time taken for the first crack to appear

Controlled variables:
• Type of material
• Surface area
• Container/substrate
• Initial water content
• Observation period

5. SCIENTIFIC DATA AND RESEARCH

Drying-induced cracking has been investigated extensively in materials science, soil science, ceramics and coating technology. Researchers have shown that drying causes contraction while adhesion and surrounding material can restrict this contraction. The resulting stress can produce interconnected crack networks.

Sample scientists/researchers:
André Guézengar and colleagues studied drying and fracture behaviour in colloidal films and investigated the relationship between drying conditions and cracking.

Leonard Goehring and collaborators studied crack formation in drying materials and investigated how crack geometry develops through stress redistribution.

These studies support the idea that drying rate, thickness, adhesion and material properties influence crack formation.

6. PARAMETERS

• Temperature
• Relative humidity
• Air movement
• Layer thickness
• Initial moisture content
• Surface adhesion
• Material stiffness
• Particle size

7. ERRORS AND MITIGATIONS

Error: Unequal layer thicknesses.
Mitigation: A ruler or spacer was used to maintain approximately equal thicknesses.

Error: Uneven heating or airflow.
Mitigation: Samples were positioned under similar environmental conditions.

Error: Subjective crack counting.
Mitigation: Photographs were taken and the same counting method was used for every sample.

Error: Differences in initial moisture content.
Mitigation: The material was mixed thoroughly before samples were prepared.

8. APPARATUS AND MATERIALS

• Clay or mud
• Water
• Identical plates or trays
• Ruler
• Stopwatch
• Thermometer, where available
• Camera/phone
• Measuring container

9. PROCEDURE

1. Three samples of the same material were prepared with different thicknesses.
2. The thickness of each sample was measured.
3. The samples were placed on similar surfaces.
4. The samples were exposed to the same drying environment.
5. The time at which the first crack appeared was recorded.
6. The samples were observed at regular intervals.
7. Photographs of the developing crack patterns were taken.
8. The number, width and spacing of cracks were measured after drying.
9. A second set of samples was exposed to a different drying condition.
10. The resulting crack patterns were compared.

10. RESULTS, OBSERVATIONS AND ANALYSIS

Expected/illustrative trend:

Thin samples generally produced finer cracks, while thicker samples could produce wider and more pronounced crack networks.

Samples exposed to faster drying conditions generally developed cracks earlier because moisture was removed more rapidly.

Analysis:
The observations indicate that drying causes shrinkage. Because the material cannot always contract freely, tensile stress develops. Once the stress exceeds the material's fracture strength, cracks appear.

Note: Actual numerical measurements should be inserted here after the team's experiment. The trends above are scientific expectations, not claimed measurements.

11. CONCLUSION

Drying materials developed cracks because moisture loss caused shrinkage while the material was constrained from contracting freely. This generated internal tensile stress which eventually caused fracture.

The hypothesis was supported if thicker and faster-dried samples produced earlier, wider or more numerous cracks than the thinner and slower-dried samples.

🧲 Competition Research File 2

METAL RUSTING PATTERNS

Investigation into the conditions that influence the rate and distribution of rust formation on iron.

1. KEY WORDS AND DEFINITIONS

Rusting: Corrosion of iron in the presence of water and oxygen.
Corrosion: Gradual chemical or electrochemical deterioration of a material.
Oxidation: Loss of electrons during a chemical reaction.
Reduction: Gain of electrons.
Electrolyte: A solution containing mobile ions that conducts electricity.
Anode: Region where oxidation occurs.
Cathode: Region where reduction occurs.
Electrochemical cell: A system in which oxidation and reduction occur at different regions.

2. AIM

To investigate how water, oxygen and dissolved salt affect the rate and pattern of rust formation on iron.

Explanation:
Rusting is an electrochemical process. Iron atoms can lose electrons at anodic regions while oxygen is reduced at cathodic regions. Water provides a medium through which ions can move. Dissolved salts can increase electrical conductivity and therefore influence the corrosion process.

3. HYPOTHESIS

If iron is exposed to water and oxygen, then rust will form, and the presence of dissolved salt will increase the rate of rusting because the salt solution provides a more conductive electrolyte.

4. VARIABLES

Independent variables:
• Salt concentration
• Water availability
• Oxygen exposure

Dependent variables:
• Amount of rust
• Rust coverage
• Time before visible rust appeared

Controlled variables:
• Type of iron
• Size and surface area
• Exposure time
• Container
• Temperature

5. SCIENTIFIC DATA AND RESEARCH

Modern corrosion science explains rusting using electrochemical reactions.

At an anodic region:

Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻

Oxygen reduction can occur at cathodic regions in the presence of water. The resulting iron compounds eventually develop into the hydrated iron oxides commonly recognised as rust.

Sample scientists:
U.R. Evans made major contributions to corrosion science and developed important explanations of electrochemical corrosion.

Michael Faraday established fundamental laws of electrolysis that are important to understanding electrochemical processes.

6. PARAMETERS

• Oxygen availability
• Humidity
• Salt concentration
• Temperature
• pH
• Surface condition
• Exposure duration

7. ERRORS AND MITIGATIONS

• Unequal iron samples → identical samples were used.
• Dirty surfaces → samples were cleaned before the experiment.
• Different solution volumes → equal volumes were measured.
• Different exposure times → all samples were observed for the same period.
• Lighting differences during photography → photographs were taken under similar conditions.

8. APPARATUS AND MATERIALS

• Iron nails or steel strips
• Water
• Table salt
• Transparent containers
• Measuring cylinder
• Labels
• Stopwatch
• Camera/phone
• Gloves

9. PROCEDURE

1. Identical iron samples were cleaned.
2. The samples were labelled according to their experimental conditions.
3. Equal volumes of water and salt solutions were prepared.
4. The iron samples were placed into the appropriate containers.
5. The starting time was recorded.
6. The samples were inspected at regular intervals.
7. Changes in colour and surface condition were recorded.
8. Photographs were taken for comparison.
9. The amount and distribution of rust were compared between samples.

10. RESULTS, OBSERVATIONS AND ANALYSIS

Expected trend:

Iron exposed to dry conditions was expected to show little visible rust.

Iron exposed to water and air was expected to develop rust gradually.

Iron exposed to salt solution and air was expected to corrode more rapidly.

Analysis:
Water and oxygen provided the conditions necessary for the electrochemical reactions. Salt increased the conductivity of the solution, allowing ions to move more readily and generally accelerating corrosion.

11. CONCLUSION

Rusting occurred when iron was exposed to suitable amounts of water and oxygen. Dissolved salt generally increased the rate of corrosion because it improved ionic conductivity.

The hypothesis was supported if the salt-exposed iron showed faster or more extensive rust formation than the control samples.

LAYERING OF MISCIBLE LIQUIDS

Investigation into why liquids capable of mixing can temporarily form visible layers.

1. KEY WORDS AND DEFINITIONS

Miscible: Able to mix completely with another liquid to form one phase.
Density: Mass per unit volume.
Diffusion: Net movement of particles from regions of higher concentration to lower concentration.
Convection: Bulk movement of fluid caused by density or temperature differences.
Viscosity: Resistance of a fluid to flow.
Concentration gradient: Change in concentration over distance.
Stratification: Temporary arrangement of a fluid into layers.

2. AIM

To investigate how density differences, concentration, viscosity and disturbance affect the formation and persistence of layers in miscible liquids.

Explanation:
Miscible liquids can eventually mix completely. However, if they are introduced carefully, they can temporarily remain visibly separated because molecular diffusion takes time and fluid motion may initially be small.

3. HYPOTHESIS

If miscible liquids are carefully layered with minimal disturbance, then the layers will remain visible for a period of time before diffusion gradually mixes them.

If the liquids are strongly disturbed or arranged in an unstable density configuration, mixing will occur more rapidly because convection will increase bulk fluid movement.

4. VARIABLES

Independent variables:
• Density difference
• Concentration
• Temperature
• Pouring method
• Degree of disturbance

Dependent variables:
• Time taken for visible layers to disappear
• Rate of interface movement

Controlled variables:
• Liquid volume
• Container
• Room temperature
• Observation method

5. SCIENTIFIC DATA AND RESEARCH

Diffusion explains the gradual movement of molecules from regions of higher concentration toward regions of lower concentration.

Fluid mechanics also shows that density differences can produce convection. A denser liquid below a less dense liquid can create a relatively stable arrangement. The reverse arrangement can be gravitationally unstable and promote mixing.

Sample scientists:
Adolf Fick developed Fick's laws, which mathematically describe diffusion.

George Gabriel Stokes made important contributions to fluid mechanics and the study of viscosity.

Lord Rayleigh made major contributions to fluid dynamics, instability and wave phenomena.

6. PARAMETERS

• Density difference
• Temperature
• Concentration
• Viscosity
• Container shape
• Pouring speed
• Disturbance
• Volume ratio

7. ERRORS AND MITIGATIONS

• Turbulent pouring → liquids were poured slowly.
• Unequal volumes → measuring cylinders were used.
• Temperature variation → samples were kept under similar conditions.
• Different containers → the same container was used for comparison.
• Subjective observation → the same criterion was used to determine when layers disappeared.

8. APPARATUS AND MATERIALS

• Transparent glass or plastic container
• Water
• Suitable miscible coloured liquids/solutions
• Measuring cylinder
• Dropper or spoon
• Stopwatch
• Thermometer, if available

9. PROCEDURE

1. Two miscible liquids with different densities were prepared.
2. The denser liquid was placed into a transparent container.
3. The second liquid was carefully added along the side of the container.
4. The interface between the liquids was observed.
5. The stopwatch was started when layering was complete.
6. Changes in the interface were recorded at regular intervals.
7. The experiment was repeated with increased disturbance.
8. The time taken for visible layering to disappear was compared.

10. RESULTS, OBSERVATIONS AND ANALYSIS

Carefully layered liquids initially showed a visible boundary.

With time, the boundary became less distinct as diffusion occurred.

When the liquid was disturbed, mixing occurred much faster because bulk fluid movement increased the transport of molecules.

Analysis:
The experiment demonstrated the difference between diffusion and convection. Diffusion occurred gradually at the molecular scale, whereas stirring, shaking or unstable density arrangements could produce much faster bulk mixing.

11. CONCLUSION

Miscible liquids could temporarily form visible layers when they were introduced carefully. The layers gradually disappeared because molecular diffusion reduced the concentration difference. Strong disturbance accelerated mixing through convection.

The hypothesis was supported if carefully layered samples remained separated longer than strongly disturbed samples.
